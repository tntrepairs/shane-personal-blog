Group Work Reflection

My experience as chairperson and technical lead of our five-member CSS group.

Download PDF

Introduction

During my first semester at Botswana Accountancy College, I served as chairperson and technical lead for our five-member Communication and Study Skills (CSS) group assignment. Our task was to develop a First-Year Student Success Plan that explored how communication, study skills, time management and teamwork could help students adjust to university life.

The group consisted of myself, Lame, Bofelo, Kenny and Angel. Although I had previously served as Assistant Head Boy at St. Joseph's College, coordinating a university assignment introduced a different kind of responsibility. I was working with fellow students who had their own academic commitments, opinions and ways of approaching tasks.

My role involved organising the overall presentation, coordinating the contributions of group members, helping maintain the required structure and taking responsibility for technical elements such as the presentation slides and practical demonstration.

Looking back, I can understand our experience through Bruce Tuckman's five stages of group development: Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing and Adjourning.

1. Forming: Establishing Our Roles and Direction

Our group began by examining what the assignment required and how we could organise ourselves to complete it. We had to prepare a presentation that was informative, practical and relevant to the challenges faced by first-year students at BAC.

During our planning meeting, all five members participated in establishing the group's responsibilities. I took on the role of chairperson and technical lead, while Lame became secretary and team process lead. Bofelo was responsible for communication, Kenny focused on study skills and time management, and Angel covered teamwork and conflict management.

This allocation of roles was important because each member had a defined area of responsibility. Rather than having everyone research the same information, we could divide the work according to the different sections of our Student Success Plan.

As chairperson, I also had to consider how the individual sections would eventually fit together. We agreed on a 12-slide presentation, including a practical demonstration video and an action plan.

At this stage, I learned that assigning responsibilities was only the beginning of leadership. I also needed to ensure that everyone understood how their contribution related to the overall assignment.

2. Storming: Managing Communication and Coordination

The Storming stage helped me reflect on the difficulties that can arise when several people are responsible for different parts of one assignment.

One challenge was coordinating contributions so that the presentation would feel like a single piece of work rather than five unrelated sections.

For example, Bofelo's section on communication, Kenny's section on time management and Angel's section on teamwork each addressed a different problem faced by students. However, these topics were closely connected. A student who receives unclear instructions may struggle to plan their time, while a group experiencing poor communication may find it difficult to collaborate effectively.

As chairperson, I had to consider these connections while organising the presentation. This required more than simply collecting everyone's material. I needed to think about the flow of the slides, avoid unnecessary repetition and ensure that the different sections supported one main message.

Another challenge was balancing my responsibilities as chairperson with my own academic workload and individual presentation tasks. I was not only coordinating the group but also preparing parts of the presentation and overseeing technical elements.

This made me appreciate the importance of delegation. My previous leadership experience gave me confidence in taking responsibility, but I also recognised that trying to manage every detail personally could become inefficient.

Our presentation itself addressed problems such as unclear communication, missed deadlines, unequal participation and disagreements. These were useful examples for considering how groups can struggle. However, I recognise that discussing these problems in our presentation is not the same as saying every one of them occurred within our own group.

The main lesson I took from this stage was that a chairperson needs to identify coordination problems early, communicate expectations clearly and distinguish between genuine disagreements and ordinary differences in working methods.

3. Norming: Establishing a Working Structure

As the work developed, our group began operating within a clearer structure. Each member had a defined topic, and the presentation plan gave us a common direction.

One of my main responsibilities was maintaining consistency across the 12 slides. Our presentation needed to move logically from the challenges faced by first-year students towards practical strategies for overcoming them.

Lame's responsibilities as secretary were particularly relevant during this stage. The assignment required evidence of the group's working process, including meeting discussions, individual contributions, responsibilities and decisions. This made record keeping an important part of our collaboration rather than something to complete only at the end.

The presentation structure also helped us bring our different topics together. Our central message was that students could improve their chances of success by learning to communicate clearly, plan their time and collaborate effectively.

I came to understand that cooperation involves more than everyone completing an individual task. Each person's work must contribute towards the same outcome, and the group must be willing to coordinate the different parts.

4. Performing: Bringing Our Work Together

The Performing stage was most visible in the practical development of our presentation.

Alongside coordinating the group, I worked on the introduction and overall presentation structure. I also took responsibility for the practical communication demonstration incorporated into Slide 9.

The demonstration contrasted an unclear message with a more effective one. For example, telling group members to send their work 'as soon as possible' provides little guidance. A clearer message identifies the task, states a specific deadline and asks the recipient to confirm their understanding.

This was one of the most useful parts of the assignment for me because it turned communication theory into a practical example. It also reinforced something I could apply in my own role as chairperson: instructions are more effective when people know exactly what is expected and when it must be completed.

Slide 10 brought the main presentation topics together through an action-plan table. This required us to consider how communication, planning and collaboration could become practical habits for first-year students.

The final slides addressed the group process, reflection and overall message. These were important because they demonstrated that the assignment was not only about explaining student success but also about applying communication and teamwork during its preparation.

Coordinating these elements gave me experience in organising content, considering how an audience would understand it and ensuring that different contributions supported the same purpose.

It also helped me distinguish between being a group leader and being the person who completes all the work. A strong group should allow each member to contribute while maintaining a shared standard of quality.

5. Adjourning: Reviewing the Group Experience

The Adjourning stage provides an opportunity to look beyond the presentation and consider what the experience taught us.

For me, the most important part of this reflection is recognising how my approach to leadership developed. At the beginning, I was primarily concerned with organising responsibilities and ensuring that the assignment requirements were covered. As the work progressed, I became more aware of the importance of communication, coordination and the relationships between different tasks.

I also recognised areas where I could improve. In future group assignments, I would establish clearer internal milestones from the beginning, check progress more systematically and create more opportunities for members to review the complete work together.

I would also aim to spend less time personally managing individual details and more time supporting accountability across the group.

Overall Reflection and Lessons Learned

Serving as chairperson was one of the more practical learning experiences of my first semester.

It gave me an opportunity to apply leadership skills I had developed at St. Joseph's College in a different academic setting. Unlike school leadership, where responsibilities often involved representing students or coordinating wider activities, this assignment required me to manage a specific academic task with defined deliverables and contributions from my peers.

I learned that leadership involves making expectations understandable, recognising the strengths of different members and ensuring that individual work contributes towards a common objective.

Tuckman's model also helped me understand that effective group work develops over time. A group needs direction during Forming, constructive problem-solving during Storming, shared working practices during Norming and coordinated effort during Performing. Adjourning then provides the opportunity to evaluate both the outcome and the process.

Most importantly, the experience reminded me that effective leadership is not measured by how many responsibilities a person takes on, but by how well they help a group work together.

I intend to carry these lessons into future university assignments and professional projects, where collaboration, communication and accountability will remain just as important as technical ability.
