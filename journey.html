An unexpected beginning

When I joined Botswana Accountancy College (BAC) in August 2026 to study Business Intelligence and Data Analytics, things were not unfolding exactly as I had originally planned. Before enrolling, I had imagined taking a different academic path, but several circumstances meant that I had to reconsider my options. Although this was not the beginning I had initially pictured, I decided to approach BAC with an open mind and make the most of the opportunity.

Adjusting to independent learning

One of the first differences I noticed was how much independence university required. At St. Joseph's College, I was accustomed to a more structured learning environment, where teachers regularly guided our progress. At BAC, lecturers introduced concepts and provided learning materials, but understanding those concepts, revising them and preparing for assessments depended largely on my own effort.

This became particularly noticeable in modules such as Statistics and Data Analysis (SDA), Computer Technology (CT) and Fundamentals of Business Finance (FBF). Each required a different approach. SDA involved understanding formulas, probability rules and calculations, while FBF required both theoretical knowledge and the ability to apply financial concepts. CT introduced technical material that also needed regular revision. I soon realised that simply attending lectures and reading notes afterwards would not always be enough.

My adjustment was not particularly smooth. I underestimated how much time independent learning would require, especially when balancing several modules, assignments and responsibilities outside the classroom. My academic performance experienced a slight decline during this period. It was disappointing because I knew I was capable of achieving better results, but I also had to acknowledge that my approach to university was not working as effectively as I wanted.

Changing my study habits

One change I made was becoming more deliberate about where and how I studied. I discovered that I was generally more productive in the BAC library than at home, where distractions could easily interrupt my concentration. I began making better use of gaps between lectures to revise, practise calculations and revisit topics I did not fully understand.

In SDA, for instance, I spent additional time practising probability, permutations and combinations, conditional probability and Venn diagrams. Rather than moving on after getting a question wrong, I began reviewing the method and attempting similar questions until I understood the reasoning. I also used practice tests to identify weaker areas instead of assuming that recognising a formula meant I could apply it correctly.

I took a similar approach to FBF by working through financial ratios, the time value of money and capital investment calculations. These exercises helped me appreciate the difference between memorising information and genuinely understanding how to use it.

Progress and lessons learned

Gradually, I became more confident in my approach to assessments and noticed signs of improvement in my understanding and practice results. I believe this reflected progress from the difficulties I had experienced earlier, although I still needed to improve my consistency.

Looking back, my first semester has taught me that university requires more than academic ability. It requires discipline, time management and the willingness to change habits that are no longer effective. I still have work to do, particularly in maintaining a consistent routine, but I now understand my responsibilities as a student much better than I did in August.

Perhaps my biggest lesson is that an unexpected change in plans does not necessarily mean a loss of direction. Although BAC was not part of the exact journey I initially imagined, it has become an environment in which I am learning to take greater responsibility for my own development.
